WOE / IV: what every line of the cell is doing

Worked on 20 borrowers · feature = income · target = default (1 = defaulted)

The goal of this cell: take one raw predictor and turn it into a small table that says, for each slice of that predictor, how much riskier or safer than average borrowers in that slice are (the WOE column) — and boil the whole variable down to a single strength score (IV). You feed WOE into the model; you use IV to decide which variables are worth keeping.

g = d.groupby('bucket')['default'].agg(['count','sum'])
g.columns = ['total','bad']

What changed: 20 rows collapse into 4 — one per bin. total = borrowers in the bin; bad = how many defaulted (sum of a 0/1 column counts the 1s).
Why: This is the heart of it. Everything from here is arithmetic on these 4 rows.

Grand totals (two plain numbers)

total_good = g['good'].sum()   # -> 10
total_bad  = g['bad'].sum()    # -> 10
value

What changed: Not a table change — just two scalars: the whole portfolio has 10 goods and 10 bads.
Why: These are the denominators for the next step. A bin's importance depends on its share of all goods/bads, so you need the totals.

Turn counts into distributions

g['dist_good'] = g['good'] / total_good
g['dist_bad']  = g['bad']  / total_bad

The .replace(0, 1e-6) guard lives on this line — it only fires when a bin has zero goods or bads (which would make the next step's log blow up to infinity). None of our bins are empty, so it does nothing here.
What changed: Each bin's share of all goods and of all bads. Row 1 holds 1/10 of goods (0.1) but 4/10 of bads (0.4).
Why: This is the trick that trips people up: WOE compares distributions, not raw counts. A bin matters if it soaks up a different slice of bads than of goods.

Weight of Evidence

g['woe'] = np.log(g['dist_good'] / g['dist_bad'])

What changed: One number per bin. Negative = more bads than goods relative to average (riskier). Positive = safer. Watch it climb monotonically as income rises.
Why: This is the value you'll swap into the model in place of raw income. By construction it's linear in the log-odds of default.

Per-bin IV contribution

g['iv_bin'] = (g['dist_good'] - g['dist_bad']) * g['woe']

What changed: How much each bin contributes to the variable's total predictive power. Extreme bins (very risky or very safe) contribute most.
Why: Summing these gives IV — the single score you rank all your variables by.

The payoff: read the WOE column as a picture

negative WOE = riskier binpositive WOE = safer bin

A clean staircase from risky (low income) to safe (high income) is exactly what you want — it means the relationship is monotonic and the binning is sensible. A jagged, up-and-down WOE usually means you over-binned.
